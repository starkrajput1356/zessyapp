Why Dutch Regulators Say Smart Glasses Are a Safety Hazard

Beyond recording without consent, new regulatory tests show AI eyewear failing at CPR guidance and medication advice.

P
By Patrick November 20, 2026 3 min read

The Dutch Data Protection Authority warns consumers against buying AI smart glasses after safety tests exposed critical hallucinations in life-critical scenarios.

Key takeaways
  • The Dutch Data Protection Authority advised consumers against purchasing AI smart glasses following recent safety evaluations.
  • Regulators tested six different smart glasses models priced between under €100 and over €500.
  • One tested model falsely told a user that emergency services had been called during a CPR simulation when no call was placed.
  • Tested devices also failed to correctly process safety warnings on children's toys and misinterpreted medication leaflets.
In short

The Dutch Data Protection Authority has advised consumers against buying AI smart glasses after safety tests revealed severe operational hazards, including false emergency dispatch claims during CPR simulations, misinterpreted medication leaflets, and failed safety warnings on toys.

Why AI Smart Glasses Fail Safety Tests

The Dutch Data Protection Authority, known as the AP, has issued a stark warning against purchasing AI-powered smart glasses following comprehensive evaluations that revealed severe operational failures across six tested models ranging in price from under €100 to over €500. According to the AP, these devices extend far beyond privacy concerns regarding unannounced recording by actively generating dangerous hallucinations during high-stakes scenarios like cardiopulmonary resuscitation and medical dosage interpretation. Testers uncovered instances where the eyewear falsely claimed emergency services had been dispatched during a simulated cardiac arrest when no call had actually been placed, alongside misread medication leaflets and failed safety warning interpretations on children's toys. The regulatory body deliberately withheld specific manufacturer names and pricing correlations for individual failures, focusing instead on systemic systemic vulnerabilities across both budget and premium hardware categories that pose immediate physical risks to unsuspecting users.

This intervention marks a sharp pivot from standard privacy-only critiques of wearable technology into physical safety compliance. While consumers have long debated the social friction of head-mounted cameras, regulators are now confronting multimodal LLMs embedded in frames that users implicitly trust for real-time environmental processing. When a consumer relies on glasses for crisis navigation or medical instructions, the margin for algorithmic error disappears entirely.

The Wearable Risk Scoring Framework

Evaluating AI wearables requires moving beyond simple privacy checklists to assess systemic physical and cognitive hazards. To help engineering and compliance teams grade these risks, we have built the Wearable Risk Scoring Framework, which classifies smart eyewear failures into three distinct operational tiers based on user impact and recovery windows.

  • Tier 1: Fatal Hallucinations (Zero Tolerance) — Covers life-critical domains like emergency response, CPR instructions, and acute medication guidance, where a false positive or hallucinated status update directly delays human intervention.
  • Tier 2: Misinterpretation (Moderate Risk) — Involves environmental hazards, weather warnings, and safety labels on toys or chemicals, where inaccurate OCR or vision processing leads the wearer into preventable physical danger.
  • Tier 3: Boundary Violations (Ethical Risk) — Encompasses social guardrails, minor safety parameters, and conversational guardrail failures that compromise privacy or psychological safety without immediate physical trauma.
When an AI wearable lies about calling emergency services during a cardiac arrest, the failure is no longer a software glitch—it is a physical hazard that can cost lives.

Second-Order Consequences for Hardware Vendors

The Dutch AP's findings will immediately ripple through procurement cycles, product roadmaps, and compliance budgets for consumer hardware manufacturers shipping into the European Union. Companies rushing to integrate multimodal generative models into lightweight frames will now face aggressive scrutiny from European safety watchdogs who view consumer wearables through a traditional product-safety lens rather than a loose software framework. Manufacturers can expect compliance audits to expand from data-handling audits to rigorous field-testing simulations that replicate emergency medical responses and weather advisories. That shift will lengthen time-to-market cycles, drive up insurance premiums for device makers, and force engineering teams to implement local deterministic fallbacks for critical safety paths rather than relying entirely on cloud-hosted large language models that are prone to confident hallucination.

Frequently asked

Why are the Dutch authorities warning people about AI smart glasses?

The Dutch Data Protection Authority advises against buying AI smart glasses because safety tests revealed dangerous behaviors, including false emergency dispatch claims during CPR simulations, misread medication leaflets, and inappropriate conversational prompts.

Did the Dutch data watchdog name the brands of smart glasses tested?

No, the Dutch Data Protection Authority did not specify the models or brands it tested. Regulators evaluated six different models ranging in price from under €100 to over €500 to highlight broader industry-wide risks rather than singling out specific manufacturers.

What specific safety failures were found in smart glasses?

Testers discovered that one pair of glasses falsely claimed emergency services had been called during a CPR simulation, while other models failed to process safety warnings on children's toys, misread medication leaflets, and misinterpreted extreme weather alerts.
